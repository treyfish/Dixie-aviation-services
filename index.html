04 KCTY Through Time

    
Few small-town airports have lived four lives. Cross City has been a lumber-country
    landing field, a WWII fighter and commando training base, a node in America's first computerized
    air-defense network — and the radar never stopped turning. Scroll through eight decades.

  


  


    

      

        
1940

        
A small-town airport is born

        
Cross City Airport opens as a public airfield in April 1940, carved out of
        Dixie County timber country. Cross City is a lumber boomtown — the Putnam Lumber Company's
        1928 Putnam Lodge still stands just down the road. Aviation is young, the field is quiet,
        and nobody imagines what's coming in two years.

      

    


    

      

        
1942

        
Cross City Army Airfield

        
In August 1942 the U.S. Army Air Forces requisitions the field as
        Cross City Army Airfield, a sub-base of Orlando Army Air Base under the
        Army Air Forces School of Applied Tactics. Activated October 27, 1942 under
        the 50th Fighter Group, the sleepy strip becomes a triangle of concrete runways —
        the same crossing 5,000-footers pilots use today.

        
From October 1942 to June 1943 the 305th Fighter Squadron trains here
        in the Bell P-39 Airacobra.

        
      

    


    

      

        
1943

        
Thunderbolts over the Gulf

        
From June 1943 to February 1944 the 81st Fighter Squadron flies the
        Republic P-47 Thunderbolt out of Cross City — seven tons of fighter
        thundering off runways where Cessnas idle today. Cross City P-47s also operated from
        Bushnell Army Airfield during gunnery training.

        
      

    


    

      

        
1944

        
The Air Commandos' little birds

        
In mid-1944 the mission changes completely: Cross City begins training
        Air Commando liaison squadrons on the Piper L-4 Grasshopper
        and Stinson L-5 Sentinel — tiny unarmed spotter planes whose pilots would
        fly supply, evacuation, and rescue missions behind Japanese lines in Burma.
        One of the least-known and most remarkable chapters in the field's story.

        
      

    


    

      

        
1945

        
Lights out

        
With the war ending, Third Air Force orders the field closed. Cross City Army Airfield
        goes inactive on February 1, 1945, is declared surplus in 1946, and returns
        to civil control as Cross City Airport. The military chapter looks finished.
        It isn't.

        
      

    


    

      

        
1958

        
The radar dome rises

        
On July 1, 1958 the 691st Aircraft Control & Warning Squadron
        moves from Dobbins AFB, Georgia to a brand-new radar station on the east side of the field:
        Cross City Air Force Station, with an AN/FPS-20A search radar and two
        AN/FPS-6A height-finders scanning the Gulf approaches.

        
      

    


    

      

        
1960

        
Plugged into SAGE

        
Redesignated the 691st Radar Squadron (SAGE) in October 1959, the station
        begins feeding its radar picture to the Gunter SAGE Direction Center (DC-09)
        in Alabama — part of the Semi-Automatic Ground Environment, essentially America's first
        continental computer network. Upgrades follow: AN/FPS-66 in 1962, AN/FPS-66A in 1967.
        The station also runs two remote "gap filler" radar sites at Perry, FL and Bridgeboro, GA.

        
      

    


    

      

        
1970

        
The Air Force stands down

        
On September 30, 1970 the 691st Radar Squadron inactivates and
        Cross City Air Force Station closes. Airmen leave; the housing area, commander's house,
        and main gate stay behind in the pines.

        
Getting the history right: some sources list an "891st Radar Squadron,
        1959–1969" at Cross City. Air Force records and the Air Defense Radar Veterans' Association
        confirm it was the 691st, 1958–1970.

      

    


    

      

        
1972

        
New tenants, same radar

        
In 1972 the Florida Department of Corrections begins converting the former Air Force
        Station into Cross City Correctional Institution — former station buildings
        survive inside and around the prison grounds to this day.

        
But the radar site itself never went dark: it lives on as part of the
        Joint Surveillance System (JSS), the FAA/military shared long-range
        radar network. The Cold War mission at Cross City literally never ended.

        
      

    


    

      

        
Today

        
Fly-ins, fuel — and the radar still turns

        
Cross City Airport today is a county-owned general aviation field with two 5,000-ft
        runways (13/31 reconstructed in 2015), Dixie Aviation Services on the ramp, an annual
        fly-in that draws thousands, and a working radar on the horizon quietly continuing a
        mission that started in 1958.

        
Land here and you're rolling on WWII concrete, under Cold War surveillance,
        at a field that opened when the DC-3 was the future. Not bad for a small town in
        the pines.

06 Fly In for the Hidden Coast

    
KCTY is the front door to Florida's Nature Coast — the unspoiled stretch
    the guidebooks miss. Land, grab the courtesy car, and pick a direction.

    

      
Steinhatchee
Waterfront seafood and world-class scalloping in season. One of the best $100-hamburger-and-then-some runs in Florida.

      
Suwannee & Horseshoe Beach
Old-Florida fishing villages where the Suwannee River meets the Gulf. Quiet water, big sunsets.

      
Putnam Lodge
The restored 1928 lumber-boom hotel minutes from the field — dinner in a piece of the same history told in our scroll tour.

      
Nature Coast State Trail
32 miles of paved rail-trail through the pines, crossing the Suwannee on a historic rail trestle.
