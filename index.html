The field guide — KCTY through time

      
Four lives of one airfield

      
Few small-town airports have lived this many. A lumber-country landing
      field, a WWII fighter and commando training base, a node in America's first computerized
      air-defense network — and a radar that never stopped turning.

    

  


  
  

    

      
1940

      

        
The field opens

        
Cross City Airport opens as a public airfield in April 1940, carved out of Dixie
        County timber country. Cross City is a lumber boomtown — the Putnam Lumber Company's
        1928 Putnam Lodge still stands just down the road. Aviation is young, the field is
        quiet, and nobody imagines what's coming in two years.

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
1942 — 1945

      
Cross City Army Airfield

    

    
    

      
In August 1942 the U.S. Army Air Forces requisitions the field as
      Cross City Army Airfield, a sub-base of Orlando Army Air Base under the
      Army Air Forces School of Applied Tactics — activated October 27, 1942 under the 50th
      Fighter Group. The sleepy strip becomes a triangle of concrete runways: the same
      crossing 5,000-footers pilots use today.

      
The 305th Fighter Squadron trains here in the Bell P-39 Airacobra
      through June 1943, followed by the 81st Fighter Squadron in the
      Republic P-47 Thunderbolt — seven tons of fighter thundering off runways where Cessnas
      idle today. In mid-1944 the mission changes completely: Cross City begins training
      Air Commando liaison squadrons on the Piper L-4 Grasshopper and Stinson
      L-5 Sentinel, tiny unarmed spotter planes whose pilots would fly supply, evacuation,
      and rescue missions behind Japanese lines in Burma.

    

    

      
      
    

    

      
With the war ending, Third Air Force orders the field closed. Cross City Army Airfield
      goes inactive on February 1, 1945, is declared surplus in 1946, and returns to civil
      control. The military chapter looks finished. It isn't.

    

  


  
  

    

      
1958 — 1970

      
The radar station and SAGE

    

    

      
      

        
On July 1, 1958 the 691st Aircraft Control & Warning Squadron moves
        from Dobbins AFB, Georgia to a new radar station on the east side of the field —
        an AN/FPS-20A search radar and two AN/FPS-6A height-finders scanning the Gulf
        approaches.

        
Redesignated the 691st Radar Squadron (SAGE) in 1959, the station feeds
        its radar picture to the Gunter SAGE Direction Center (DC-09) in Alabama — part of
        the Semi-Automatic Ground Environment, essentially America's first continental
        computer network. Upgrades follow (AN/FPS-66 in 1962, FPS-66A in 1967), plus two
        remote "gap filler" sites at Perry, Florida and Bridgeboro, Georgia.

      

    

    

      
      

        
On September 30, 1970 the 691st inactivates and the station closes. Airmen leave;
        the housing area, commander's house, and main gate stay behind in the pines.

        
A note on the record Some sources
        list an "891st Radar Squadron, 1959–1969" at Cross City. Air Force records and the
        Air Defense Radar Veterans' Association confirm it was the 691st, 1958–1970.

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
1972 — Today

      
Civil aviation, and a radar that still turns

    

    

      
      
    

    

      
In 1972 the Florida Department of Corrections begins converting the former Air Force
      Station into Cross City Correctional Institution — former station buildings
      survive inside and around the prison grounds to this day. But the radar site itself never
      went dark: it operates on as part of the Joint Surveillance System, the
      FAA/military shared long-range radar network.

      
Today the field is a county-owned general aviation airport — two 5,000-ft runways
      (13/31 rebuilt in 2015), Dixie Aviation Services on the ramp, an annual fly-in, and a
      working radar on the horizon quietly continuing a mission that began in 1958. Land here
      and you're rolling on WWII concrete, under Cold War surveillance, at a field that opened
      when the DC-3 was the future.

Fly in for the Hidden Coast

      
KCTY is the front door to the unspoiled stretch of Gulf coast the
      guidebooks miss. Land, grab the courtesy car, and pick a direction.

    


    

      
Annual Fly-In, Cruise-In & Business Expo

      
Every spring the Dixie County Chamber of Commerce turns the ramp into the county's
      biggest gathering — past events have drawn 3,500+ visitors,
      65 aircraft, and 100 local businesses, with helicopter
      rides, Coast Guard flyovers, and classic cars. Watch
      Dixie County and
      Chamber announcements for the next date, or call the FBO and we'll tell you.

The airport and the county

    
Flight training, corporate aviation, agricultural spraying,
    medical transport, and search-and-rescue staging — Cross City Airport generates
    $7.7M in annual economic output, supports 50 jobs and
    $2.4M in payroll, and handled 18,954 operations in 2023.

    
Source: FDOT Florida Aviation System Plan 2043 airport profile (2023 data).
    2043 forecast: 16 based aircraft, ~24,900 annual operations.
